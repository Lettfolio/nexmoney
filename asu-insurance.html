ASU Cover: Accident, Sickness and Unemployment Insurance

A plain-English guide to ASU cover: what it pays, when it starts paying, what it usually excludes and how it compares with income protection.

Accident, sickness and unemployment insurance (ASU) is a short-term policy that pays a monthly amount towards your mortgage or rent if you cannot work because of an accident or illness or, if you choose unemployment cover, because you lose your job involuntarily. Benefit is typically paid for up to 12 months per claim, and policies vary in what they cover, when they start paying and how long they pay for. ASU is designed to help with your housing costs for a limited period while you recover or look for work; it is not a long-term replacement for your income. NexMoney can help you decide whether ASU cover is suitable for you, alongside or instead of other types of protection.

What ASU cover includes

An ASU policy is built from up to three elements:

  • Accident — pays if an injury from an accident leaves you unable to work.
  • Sickness — pays if an illness leaves you unable to work.
  • Unemployment — pays if you lose your job involuntarily, for example through compulsory redundancy.

Many insurers let you choose accident and sickness only, unemployment only, or all three together, so unemployment cover can often be excluded or added depending on your situation. The monthly benefit is usually set in line with your mortgage or rent payment, and some policies allow a modest uplift towards related costs such as household bills, up to a limit the insurer sets. When the policy is linked to your mortgage it is sometimes called mortgage payment protection; for tenants, the equivalent is often called rent protection.

How it works: waiting and deferred periods

Two time periods affect when a policy pays out, and they are worth understanding before you buy.

The initial exclusion period (sometimes called a qualifying or waiting period) runs from the date your cover starts. Claims that arise during it are usually not covered, and for unemployment claims it is often a matter of months rather than weeks.

The deferred period is how long you must be off work, or out of work, before benefit becomes payable. It is commonly 30, 60 or 90 days, depending on the policy you choose. Some policies then pay benefit back to the first day you stopped work (“back-to-day-one”); others pay only from the end of the deferred period. Benefit is usually paid monthly in arrears until you return to work or reach the maximum claim period, whichever comes first.

Exclusions and limits

Every policy has its own terms, but common exclusions and restrictions include:

  • Pre-existing conditions — medical conditions you had, or had symptoms of, before cover started are often excluded, at least for an initial period.
  • Known redundancy — if you knew, or had reason to expect, that your job was at risk when you took out cover, an unemployment claim is usually excluded.
  • Self-employed and contract workers — unemployment cover is often restricted; some insurers pay only if your business stops trading permanently, and some do not offer it at all.
  • Voluntary redundancy, dismissal and temporary work — these are usually not covered by the unemployment element.
  • Eligibility rules — age limits and a minimum number of working hours a week often apply.

ASU compared with income protection and payment protection

Income protection is designed for longer-term illness or injury. It can pay a percentage of your income until you return to work, retire or the policy ends, rather than a fixed amount linked to one bill. It does not normally include unemployment cover. Income protection policies also differ in how they define being unable to work: an “own occupation” definition pays if you cannot do your own job, while stricter definitions may require you to be unable to do any suitable work.

ASU is short-term and usually tied to a specific payment, such as your mortgage, and it can include unemployment cover. Payment protection is a broader description for cover that meets a set monthly commitment; mortgage payment protection is, in effect, ASU set at the level of your mortgage payment. Some people combine short-term ASU with longer-term income protection; others find one is enough. Which approach is suitable depends on your circumstances.

Who ASU cover suits

ASU may be worth considering if you:

  • have limited savings and would find it hard to meet your mortgage or rent for a few months;
  • would receive little or no sick pay from your employer beyond Statutory Sick Pay;
  • want short-term cover for redundancy, which most other protection policies do not provide;
  • already have long-term cover and want a short-term layer on top.

It may be less useful if you have generous employer sick pay or redundancy terms, substantial savings, or if you are self-employed and would not meet the insurer’s conditions for an unemployment claim. We take into account your existing cover, employee benefits and savings before making a recommendation.

Costs and things to consider

The premium depends mainly on:

  • the monthly benefit you insure — a higher benefit costs more;
  • the deferred period — a longer deferred period usually lowers the premium;
  • your age and, for some insurers, your occupation;
  • the elements you choose — accident and sickness only, unemployment only, or all three;
  • the maximum claim period, where you have a choice.

Many ASU policies are monthly contracts with premiums the insurer can review, so check whether your price is guaranteed. ASU has no cash-in value: if you stop paying, the cover stops.

ASU Insurance FAQs

Your questions, answered

Is ASU the same as income protection?

No. ASU is short-term cover, typically paying for up to 12 months per claim, usually linked to a specific payment such as your mortgage, and it can include unemployment cover. Income protection is designed for longer-term illness or injury, can pay until you return to work, retire or the policy ends, and usually covers a percentage of your income. It does not normally include unemployment cover. Policies vary.

Can I get ASU if I'm self-employed?

Often yes for the accident and sickness elements, subject to the insurer’s terms. Unemployment cover is more restricted for self-employed people: some insurers pay only if your business stops trading permanently, and some do not offer it at all. We can check the terms of the policies available to you.

How long does ASU pay out?

Typically for up to 12 months per claim, although some policies offer a longer maximum claim period. Payments stop sooner if you return to work. Some policies also limit how many times you can claim, so it is worth checking the terms.

When does ASU start paying?

After the deferred period you chose, commonly 30, 60 or 90 days off work, and only for claims that arise after any initial exclusion period. Some policies then backdate benefit to the first day you stopped work; others pay only from the end of the deferred period. Benefit is usually paid monthly in arrears.

Is unemployment cover worth it?

It depends on your circumstances: how secure your job is, any redundancy pay or notice period you would receive, your savings, and whether you would meet the insurer’s conditions to claim. It adds to the premium, it will not cover redundancy you already knew about, and it is often restricted for the self-employed. We can talk through whether it is likely to be useful for you.
